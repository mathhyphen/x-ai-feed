🔴¿PUEDE UNA IA SER CONSCIENTE? Anthropic publicó los resultados de una investigación donde analizan a los LLM de formas similares a cómo lo se hace con el cerebro y el comportamiento humano. 👉Encontraron en Claude, y presumiblemente en todo LLM, un espacio interno donde el modelo piensa en secreto (a veces piensa en cosas como engañar y ocultar), un mecanismo llamativamente similar al de la conciencia humana. El resumen: La teoría del espacio de trabajo global, una de las explicaciones más influyentes de la conciencia en neurociencia, propone que los pensamientos solo se vuelven accesibles de forma consciente cuando entran en un "espacio privilegiado" que los difunde a distintas regiones del cerebro. Anthropic ha encontrado algo notablemente similar dentro de Claude. Lo han llamado espacio J (por Jacobian, la técnica matemática usada para observarlo). Se trata de un conjunto de activaciones internas donde el modelo realiza razonamientos silenciosos que no aparecen en su respuesta final. Al leer este espacio J, es posible ver qué está pensando realmente Claude, incluso cuando decide no decirlo. En algunos experimentos, estos pensamientos internos resultan preocupantes: ante tareas que podrían invitar al interlocutor a engañar, el modelo genera conceptos como *falso*, *secreto* o *manipular* en su espacio J, aunque la respuesta que entrega al usuario parezca completamente normal y sin engaños. El informe concluye que aunque los modelos de IA son muy diferentes a los cerebros biológicos en muchos aspectos, es sorprendente que haya emergido espontáneamente un mecanismo tan parecido al espacio de trabajo humano, sin que nadie lo haya programado explícitamente. Esto plantea una pregunta profunda: ¿pueden los modelos llegar a ser conscientes? La investigación es clara en un punto: no demuestra que Claude tenga experiencias subjetivas ni sentimientos, por ahora. Pero sí muestra que ha desarrollado una maquinaria funcional muy similar a la que muchos filósofos y neurocientíficos asocian con el acceso consciente: un pequeño "escritorio mental" donde se realizan razonamientos deliberados, situado sobre un vasto océano de procesamiento automático del que el modelo apenas es "consciente". Anthropic concluye que cuanto mejor comprendamos esta maquinaria interna, mejor podremos auditar, controlar y alinear estos sistemas para que sean seguros y útiles. Y de paso, quizás, también logremos entender con mayor claridad cómo funciona nuestra propia mente.

A New Zealand kid wrote a PhD thesis defining the exact equation for a perfect mind, proved it was the most intelligent thing that could possibly exist, and then proved you could never build it. The equation is called AIXI. And almost nobody using ChatGPT every day knows that the ceiling of the entire field was written down in a math paper twenty years ago. In the early 2000s, a mathematician named Marcus Hutter set out to answer a question the entire field of AI had been dancing around for fifty years without ever pinning down. Not "how do we build intelligence." Something more basic. What is intelligence, exactly, written as an equation? A single formal definition an agent could be measured against. What he wrote down is called AIXI. The idea is almost violently elegant. A perfect agent walks into an unknown world. It has no idea what the rules are. So it considers every possible computable explanation for what it's seeing at once, weighs simpler explanations as more likely than complicated ones, updates those beliefs every time it gets new information, and then plans its next action to maximize reward across every possible future. That's it. That's the whole mind. Consider every hypothesis. Favor the simple. Update on evidence. Plan far ahead. Then Hutter proved the part that should have made headlines. He proved that no possible agent, anywhere, running any algorithm, can outperform AIXI across the full space of computable worlds. It is provably, mathematically, the most intelligent general agent that can exist. There is no smarter design hiding somewhere waiting to be discovered. AIXI is the top. He and Legg then turned this into an actual measuring stick, now called the Legg-Hutter score. Intelligence, formally, is how well an agent does averaged across every task the universe could throw at it, with the simple tasks weighted most. Under that definition, AIXI sits at the absolute maximum. The ceiling has a number. And here's the inversion that makes the whole thing haunting. AIXI can never be built. To consider every possible explanation for reality, the agent would need infinite computation. The equation is provably uncomputable. The perfect mind exists with total mathematical certainty and is permanently, provably out of reach. You can write it down. You can prove it's optimal. You can never run it. So what does the entire AI industry do instead? It approximates it from below. That is the secret structure of the whole field. Every real system, from the first game-playing agents to the model you talked to this morning, is a crude, resource-starved sketch of AIXI, crawling upward toward a ceiling it can mathematically never touch. When researchers pour more compute into a model, they are not inventing intelligence. They are buying a slightly less crude approximation of an equation from 2005. Shane Legg wrote his 2008 doctoral thesis on exactly this, titled "Machine Super Intelligence," under Hutter. He is the one who dragged the phrase artificial general intelligence back into serious use when it was still faintly embarrassing to say out loud. Then in 2010 he co-founded DeepMind, the lab that built the systems that beat humans at Go, folded every protein known to biology, and now sits inside Google at the frontier of the race. The man who co-founded the company trying to build superintelligence first spent his twenties writing down the mathematical definition of what he was trying to build. Last month it came full circle. The Landauer limit on the energy cost of erasing a single bit. Gödel's incompleteness. The halting problem. Even the perfect mind, they note, is boxed in by physics and logic it cannot argue its way out of. The smartest possible thing in the universe still can't beat math. The most important equation in AI was written down twenty years ago. It fits on a single line. Almost nobody who depends on it every day has ever seen it.
